# Chapter 9 — A Small Kind of Courage

Emily decided to enter the writing showcase on a Tuesday.

She did not announce the decision.

She simply opened the application form during study period, filled in her name, and pressed submit before she could reconsider.

When she told Sarah after school, Sarah reacted as if Emily had announced an engagement.

“You did it!”

“It's only an application.”

“It's a commitment.”

“It's a school showcase.”

“It's a public declaration of literary ambition.”

Emily laughed.

“You make everything sound enormous.”

“Someone has to.”

Michael heard about it later that afternoon.

They were in the library, sitting at their usual table. Emily had brought a folder of possible topics, while Michael was trying to finish a set of mathematics problems.

“You entered?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He put down his pencil.

“That's great.”

“Don't make it a big thing.”

“I'm not.”

“You look pleased.”

“I am.”

Emily opened her folder.

“I haven't chosen a subject yet.”

“What are you considering?”

“Something about the way people remember places.”

Michael leaned forward.

“Like your photographs?”

“Maybe. I want to explore how a place can mean different things to different people. A house can be a home to one person and just a building to someone else.”

He nodded.

“That's interesting.”

“I also thought about interviewing people.”

“Your parents?”

“Possibly. And maybe some of the older people in the neighborhood.”

Michael looked thoughtful.

“My mother would talk for hours.”

Emily smiled.

“I've noticed.”

“She has stories about everyone.”

“Especially your father?”

“Especially my father.”

They laughed.

Michael closed his mathematics book.

“If you want, I can help you think through the structure.”

“Wouldn't that take time away from your own work?”

“Probably.”

“Then why offer?”

He looked at her as if the answer were obvious.

“Because I want to.”

Emily lowered her eyes to the folder.

“Thank you.”

***

The following weekend, Emily visited the Reed house with Elizabeth.

The original plan had been for the mothers to spend an afternoon together. Margaret had invited them for lunch, and Elizabeth had accepted before Emily could invent an excuse to stay home.

Emily was not unhappy about going.

She was simply aware that Michael would be there.

When they arrived, Margaret opened the door.

“Elizabeth!”

The two women embraced.

Then Margaret turned toward Emily.

“Come in. Michael's in the living room, and Ashley is pretending to study.”

From somewhere inside the house, Ashley called, “I can hear you!”

Margaret smiled.

“Good. Then you know you're included.”

Emily followed Elizabeth inside.

Michael appeared at the end of the hallway.

“Hi.”

“Hi.”

He glanced at her coat.

“Cold outside?”

“A little.”

He reached for the coat.

“Let me take that.”

She handed it to him.

“Thank you.”

Ashley appeared behind him.

“Hello, Emily.”

“Hi, Ashley.”

“Come on. I want to show you something.”

Michael looked at his sister.

“What?”

“Something.”

“That's not an explanation.”

“It's not for you.”

Ashley took Emily's hand and led her toward the sitting room.

***

The something turned out to be a collection of old family photographs.

Ashley had found a box while looking for a school document. It contained pictures from holidays, birthdays, and family gatherings.

“This is Michael when he was seven,” she said.

Emily looked at the photograph.

Michael was standing in a garden wearing a striped shirt, holding a toy airplane. His hair was too long, and his expression was solemn.

“He looks serious.”

“He thought he was an engineer.”

From the doorway, Michael objected.

“I was interested in aviation.”

“You were seven.”

“I had interests.”

Ashley turned another photograph toward Emily.

This one showed Michael sitting on the floor surrounded by cardboard boxes and pieces of tape.

“He built an airport in the living room,” Ashley explained.

Michael sighed.

“It was a model.”

“It blocked the television for three days.”

Emily laughed.

Michael came closer and took the photograph.

“I had to test the runway.”

“By driving a toy car over it?” Ashley asked.

“It was a technical test.”

Emily looked at him.

“You've always been like this.”

“Like what?”

“Building things out of whatever you can find.”

He smiled.

“Apparently.”

Ashley continued turning the pages.

There were pictures of Michael with his parents, with David at a baseball game, with Margaret at the beach, and with Ashley at different stages of childhood.

Emily noticed that Margaret appeared in nearly every group photograph with a broad, unmistakable smile.

“She always looks happy,” Emily said.

Ashley looked at the picture.

“She is. Or she used to be even louder.”

Michael nodded.

“She still is.”

“Not when she's trying to be quiet,” Ashley replied.

He looked at his sister.

“That's not a thing.”

“It is. She whispers at full volume.”

Emily laughed.

The ordinary warmth of the family was easy to see.

***

Lunch was served in the dining room.

Margaret had made too much food, and David insisted that this was not an accident but a long-standing family policy.

“Your mother believes every guest is secretly starving,” he told Emily.

Margaret placed another dish on the table.

“It's better than having someone leave hungry.”

Elizabeth nodded.

“She's always been like that.”

“You remember the school picnic?” Margaret asked.

Elizabeth groaned.

“Which one?”

“The one where you packed enough food for an entire sports team.”

“I was being prepared.”

“You brought three cakes.”

“One was for the teachers.”

“The teachers were two people.”

“They were very hungry people.”

David looked at James Bennett.

“You see what I live with?”

James smiled.

“I suspect you have learned to adapt.”

“Over many years.”

The table laughed.

Michael sat beside Emily. Their elbows touched once when they reached for the same bowl.

“Sorry,” he said.

“It's fine.”

He moved his arm slightly, giving her more room.

She noticed the small gesture.

***

After lunch, the adults settled in the sitting room with tea.

Emily and Michael went to the back garden.

The air was cold but clear. A few leaves remained on the trees, and the grass was damp from the previous night's rain.

Michael pointed toward a narrow wooden structure near the fence.

“I built that when I was younger.”

Emily examined it.

“What is it?”

“A model bridge.”

“It looks like a bridge.”

“Thank you.”

“Does it work?”

He picked up a small wooden block and placed it on the center.

The structure held.

Emily smiled.

“Impressive.”

“It took three attempts.”

“Only three?”

He looked offended.

“I was a child.”

She laughed.

They stood beside it for a while.

Then Michael asked, “What are you writing about?”

Emily explained her idea.

He listened without interrupting.

When she finished, he said, “You should include the people who make a place feel like home.”

She looked at him.

“That's a good point.”

“It's not always the building.”

“No.”

“Sometimes it's who is waiting inside.”

Emily thought about the photograph of the old doorway.

“That's what I want to understand.”

Michael looked toward the house.

“My mother would say a home is wherever everyone ends up in the kitchen.”

Emily smiled.

“She's probably right.”

He nodded.

“She usually is. Don't tell her I said that.”

“I won't.”

They laughed.

***

Inside, Margaret and Elizabeth had found another photograph album.

This one was from their final year at school.

Elizabeth turned a page and stopped.

“Oh, no.”

Margaret leaned over.

“What?”

Elizabeth showed her a photograph of the two of them standing beside a noticeboard covered in handwritten announcements.

Margaret stared.

“We thought we were being subtle.”

“We were not.”

The photograph showed both girls wearing identical scarves, despite having agreed that morning not to dress alike.

“We did that on purpose,” Margaret said.

“You told me you hated matching clothes.”

“I did.”

“Then why did you buy the same scarf?”

“Because you bought yours first.”

Elizabeth laughed.

They remembered the afternoon they had spent trying to organize a surprise birthday party, only to invite the birthday girl by mistake. They remembered a school trip where Margaret had forgotten her suitcase and Elizabeth had shared half her clothes. They remembered a teacher who had once threatened to separate them because they could not stop whispering.

For a while, the room filled with laughter.

Then Margaret closed the album.

“It's strange,” she said. “How much of life happens before you know which moments you'll miss.”

Elizabeth reached for her hand.

“I think that's why we have to pay attention while we're in them.”

Margaret nodded.

From the garden came the sound of Emily laughing.

Both women looked toward the window.

Margaret smiled.

“She's good for him.”

Elizabeth looked at her.

“He's good for her too.”

They left it there.

***

When it was time to go, Michael walked Emily and Elizabeth to the door.

Ashley called goodbye from the stairs.

Margaret hugged Elizabeth, then Emily.

“Come again soon.”

“I will,” Emily said.

Michael stood beside the doorway while Emily put on her coat.

“Did you enjoy the photographs?” he asked.

“Yes. Your childhood was very well documented.”

“Unfortunately.”

“I liked the airport.”

“It was a serious project.”

“I could tell.”

He smiled.

Then he looked at her.

“Send me your outline when you start writing. I'll read it.”

“Are you sure?”

“Of course.”

“Be honest.”

“I will.”

“Even if it's terrible?”

“Especially if it's terrible.”

She laughed.

“That's reassuring.”

He opened the door.

“See you at school.”

“See you.”

***

On the drive home, Elizabeth was quiet for a few minutes.

Then she said, “I like Margaret.”

Emily smiled.

“You've known her for years.”

“People can become different versions of themselves. It's nice to discover that the good parts are still there.”

Emily looked out the window.

“Michael's family is lovely.”

“They are.”

Elizabeth glanced at her.

“And you seem very comfortable there.”

Emily pretended to study the passing houses.

“I was comfortable.”

“Good.”

A few moments passed.

Then Elizabeth added, “You know, you don't have to be embarrassed about liking someone.”

Emily turned toward her.

“Mom.”

“I'm not asking you to tell me anything. I'm just saying.”

Emily looked down at her hands.

“I like spending time with him.”

Elizabeth smiled.

“That's a good place to start.”

***

That night, Emily sent Michael the first page of her outline.

He replied twelve minutes later.

**Michael:** I like the opening question.

**Emily:** Only the opening question?

**Michael:** I haven't finished reading.

**Emily:** Fair.

A minute later:

**Michael:** The idea about the people inside a place is strong. Keep that.

**Emily:** You said that today.

**Michael:** I meant it today too.

Emily smiled.

**Emily:** Thank you for reading it.

**Michael:** Thank you for sending it.

She was about to put her phone away when another message appeared.

**Michael:** I had a good afternoon.

Emily looked at the screen.

**Emily:** Me too.

**Michael:** Goodnight, Emily.

**Emily:** Goodnight.

She set the phone on her bedside table.

For a long moment, she watched the quiet room around her.

There were books on the shelf, photographs above the desk, and the familiar outline of the house beyond her bedroom door.

Nothing had changed.

And yet the world felt a little larger than it had that morning.

Perhaps that was what courage looked like at the beginning: not a grand declaration, but the willingness to let someone become important.
