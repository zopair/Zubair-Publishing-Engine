# Chapter 16: A Season of Small Goodbyes

By the end of summer, Emily had learned that a goodbye could be almost invisible.

It could be Michael stepping onto a train after a Saturday together. It could be the end of a phone call when neither wanted to be the first to hang up. It could be the last afternoon of the engineering program, when he packed his notebooks into a bag and realized that the studio, which had seemed unfamiliar in June, had become a place he would miss.

The summer had changed them in small ways. Michael had become more willing to ask questions before defending an idea. Emily had learned to read her work aloud without apologizing for it. They had missed each other, but they had not turned missing into a contest.

On the final Friday, Michael came home with a cardboard tube containing his presentation drawings and a small paper bag from the bakery near the studio.

“For you,” he said when he met Emily at the station.

She peered into the bag. “A pastry?”

“A carefully selected pastry.”

“What makes it carefully selected?”

“I considered several candidates.”

“Did you interview them?”

“One was overconfident.”

She laughed and took his hand.

They walked to the river, where the late-summer light made the water look almost metallic. Michael told her about the final review, the instructor who had praised his revisions, and a student who had designed a modular shelter for people waiting at bus stops.

“I think I'd like to work on public projects,” he said. “Things people use without having to think about them.”

“Like a good bridge.”

“Or a bench in the right place. Or a station entrance that doesn't make someone feel lost.”

Emily considered that. “You like making the world easier to move through.”

“I suppose I do.”

“You should keep that.”

He looked at her. “I intend to.”

They sat on a low wall and shared the pastry. It was filled with apricot jam, which Michael claimed was superior to every other filling. Emily disagreed on principle.

“You're only saying that because you want to argue,” he said.

“Sometimes the argument is the point.”

“I know. It's one of your more alarming qualities.”

She leaned against him. “You missed me.”

“Terribly.”

“Even when I sent you photographs of my breakfast?”

“Especially then. I began to understand how many things you photograph before eating.”

“It's called documentation.”

“It's called letting toast get cold.”

They laughed until a jogger passed and gave them a curious look.

The next week, school preparations began. Emily and Sarah sorted their schedules, bought notebooks, and debated whether senior year would be as difficult as everyone claimed. Sophia had decided to help organize the autumn arts night. Olivia was preparing for a debate competition. Madison had been selected to coordinate a volunteer project, and she asked Emily whether the photography club might document it.

Their lives were widening. The circle of people around them was becoming more complicated, but also more generous.

At the first school gathering, Emily found Madison arranging name cards on a table. A stack had fallen to the floor.

“Need help?” Emily asked.

Madison looked up. “Please. I thought I had a system.”

“You do. The cards are simply refusing to respect it.”

They collected the cards together. For a few minutes they worked in comfortable silence.

“Michael told me the summer program went well,” Madison said.

“It did. He learned a lot.”

“He seemed happy when he presented.”

“He was.”

Madison hesitated. “I hope you know I wasn't trying to make things difficult last spring.”

Emily looked at her. “I don't think you were.”

“I liked talking to him. I admired his work. And, yes, I thought he was attractive. But I knew he was with you.”

“Thank you for saying that.”

Madison gave a small, relieved smile. “I didn't want you to think every conversation was a competition.”

Emily handed her the last name card. “I don't want that either.”

They returned to arranging the table. The exchange did not make them close friends, but it made the space between them less crowded.

Later, Michael arrived with a box of display stands. He greeted Madison, then Emily, and asked where the stands should go.

“Over there,” Madison said. “Emily has already rescued my system.”

“She's good at that,” he replied.

Emily raised an eyebrow. “Rescuing systems?”

“People. Projects. My breakfast habits.”

Madison laughed. “That sounds useful.”

Michael carried the box to the display area. Emily watched him go, then turned back to Madison.

“He does need help with breakfast,” she said.

“I believe you.”

That evening, the families met for dinner at the Reed house. Margaret had made too much food, as usual. Elizabeth brought a pie, and James helped David repair a loose hinge on the garden gate. Ashley had returned from a short trip with stories that grew more dramatic each time she told them.

At the table, Margaret asked Michael whether he had decided on university applications.

“I have a list,” he said.

“A list is not a decision,” Ashley observed.

“Thank you for your contribution.”

“I'm here to help.”

Emily listened as the conversation moved between universities, scholarships, and possible courses. The future was no longer an abstract subject. It had dates and applications and train routes.

Later, Margaret found Emily in the kitchen, rinsing glasses.

“You've been quiet tonight,” she said.

“Just thinking.”

“About the future?”

Emily nodded.

Margaret dried a plate. “It can feel as though every decision is a door that closes behind you. But most lives are made of doors that open again in unexpected places.”

“Did you know what you wanted at our age?”

Margaret laughed. “I wanted to be a singer, a pilot, and the owner of a little bookshop. I achieved none of those things.”

“Do you regret it?”

“Not the way you mean. I found other lives I hadn't imagined. That's the trick. You don't have to know the whole story before you turn the page.”

Emily looked at her. “You make it sound easy.”

“It isn't. But it is possible.”

On the way home, Michael walked Emily to her door. The air had turned cooler. He tucked his hands into his jacket pockets.

“Are you all right?” he asked.

“I'm thinking about how quickly everything is changing.”

“It's not all changing at once.”

“Sometimes it feels that way.”

He took her hand. “Then we'll take it one thing at a time.”

She leaned into him. “I don't want us to become people who only remember how things used to be.”

“We won't.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I want to know what you become next.”

She looked up at him. The sentence was not grand, but it held a kind of faith she could understand.

“Then I want to know what you become too,” she said.

He kissed her, and they stood beneath the porch light until the cold persuaded them to say goodnight.

When Emily went inside, she found a message from Sarah asking whether she was ready for senior year.

Emily typed: *Not entirely. But I think I'm ready to begin.*

She looked once more through the window. Michael was halfway down the path, walking toward the street with his shoulders slightly hunched against the wind.

A season was ending. Another was waiting.

She no longer thought every goodbye had to be a loss. Some were simply the space between one meeting and the next.
