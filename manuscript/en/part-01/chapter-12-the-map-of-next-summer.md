# Chapter 12: The Map of Next Summer

The acceptance email arrived on a Tuesday morning, while Michael was standing in the school library pretending to read a book about structural design.

He had checked his inbox so many times that the librarian had begun looking at him with professional suspicion. Emily sat across the table with her writing notebook open, trying not to watch him watch his phone.

“You're going to wear a hole through the screen,” she said.

“I think the screen is designed for this.”

“Not for anxious staring.”

“Then the manufacturer has failed me.”

His phone vibrated.

He looked down.

For one second, nothing moved. Then his eyes widened, and he read the message again, slowly, as if the words might rearrange themselves if he blinked.

“What?” Emily asked.

He turned the phone toward her.

The summer engineering program had accepted him.

Emily's smile came before the words. “Michael!”

He stood so quickly that his chair scraped the floor. The librarian looked up. He sat down again, remembering where they were, but his grin refused to become smaller.

“You got in,” Emily whispered.

“I got in.”

She reached across the table and squeezed both his hands. “I knew you would.”

“You said that before you knew.”

“I was confident.”

“You were reckless.”

“I was right.”

He laughed, and for a moment the library seemed to hold only the two of them and the small bright fact on his screen.

Then the practical questions arrived. The program would run for six weeks in the city. There would be early trains, long studio days, a final presentation, and a schedule that would leave less room for the ordinary afternoons they had begun to depend on.

Michael read the details aloud. Emily listened, asking about the commute and the project requirements. She was genuinely excited for him. She was also aware of a quiet ache gathering beneath her happiness.

He noticed.

“Hey,” he said. “Look at me.”

She did.

“I want this,” he said. “But I don't want you to think it means I'm moving away from you.”

“I don't think that.”

“You looked like you were doing complicated arithmetic.”

“I was calculating how many times I can complain about your early train.”

“An important figure.”

She smiled, and he brushed his thumb across her knuckles.

“We'll work it out,” she said.

“Together?”

“Together.”

They celebrated after school at a small diner near the station. Michael ordered pancakes, then insisted that the occasion required a second order of fries. Emily told him that engineering programs did not normally recommend celebratory carbohydrates. He replied that he was prepared to challenge the curriculum.

When the food arrived, he placed one of the fries on her plate.

“An offering,” he said.

“Is it poisoned?”

“Only with happiness.”

“That sounds medically concerning.”

He leaned back, laughing. Then his expression softened.

“I wouldn't have applied if you hadn't pushed me.”

“You would have. Maybe later.”

“Maybe. But you made it feel possible.”

Emily looked down at the table. “You make things feel possible for me too.”

He reached across the space between them. Their hands met beside the ketchup bottle.

That evening, the two families gathered at the Reed house to celebrate. Margaret had prepared dinner, and David opened a bottle of sparkling cider. Ashley made a small paper sign that read ENGINEER IN TRAINING and taped it to Michael's bedroom door. Michael objected to the font, which Ashley declared to be proof that he was already becoming unbearable.

Elizabeth arrived with a cake. James brought a small notebook for Michael to use during the program. Olivia sent a message asking for photographs of the final presentation, while Sarah demanded that Emily document the entire summer as if she were reporting on an expedition.

Madison sent Michael a short congratulatory message and a link to an exhibition she thought he might enjoy. He thanked her, then put his phone away and returned to the dinner conversation.

After the meal, the adults drifted toward the kitchen. Ashley went upstairs to find an old photograph. Emily stepped onto the back porch, where the evening had cooled enough to make the warm light from the windows feel inviting.

Michael followed with two mugs of tea.

“You disappeared,” he said.

“I wanted a minute.”

He handed her a mug and stood beside her. The garden was small, bordered by a wooden fence and a row of hydrangeas that Margaret tended with the devotion of a person raising a second family.

“Are you worried?” he asked.

“A little.”

“About the program?”

“About missing you. About being proud of you and selfish at the same time.”

Michael leaned against the railing. “I think those feelings can live in the same room.”

“You've been speaking to my mother.”

“No. Your mother uses longer sentences.”

Emily laughed.

He took a folded sheet of paper from his jacket. It was a rough calendar, marked with the program dates, train times, and a list of weekends.

“I thought we could make a plan,” he said. “Not a strict one. Just something that gives us things to look forward to.”

She opened it. He had marked possible Saturday visits, one evening when they could meet halfway, and the date of the final presentation. In the margin he had written: *One pancake emergency, to be used when necessary.*

“You made a map,” she said.

“I make bridges. Maps are a natural progression.”

She traced the dates with her finger. “You didn't have to do this.”

“I wanted to.”

“It's thoughtful.”

“Don't sound so surprised.”

“I am surprised. You usually draw on the backs of receipts.”

“Receipts are an underrated medium.”

She folded the paper carefully and put it in her bag.

“I'll come to the final presentation,” she said.

“I was hoping you would.”

“And I'll visit when I can.”

“I'll meet you at the station.”

“Even if it means waking up early?”

“Especially then. I want you to see how impressive I look before coffee.”

“You look like a confused owl before coffee.”

“That's still impressive in certain circles.”

She laughed, and he stepped closer. For a while they stood without speaking. From inside came the sound of Margaret laughing at something Elizabeth had said. The warmth of it crossed the open door and settled around them.

Michael looked toward the house. “I used to think growing up meant getting far enough away to make all your own decisions.”

“And now?”

“Now I think it means learning which people you want beside you when you make them.”

Emily felt the words settle into her. She set down her mug and turned toward him.

“I want to be beside you,” she said. “Not in the way of what you want. Beside it.”

His face changed. The playful expression gave way to something quieter.

“That's exactly where I want you.”

He kissed her, slowly and without hurry. It was not their first kiss, but it carried the weight of a new promise: not that the summer would be easy, or that they would never miss one another, but that neither of them intended to pretend distance was the same as disinterest.

When they went back inside, Margaret was waiting near the kitchen door.

“Have you two solved the future?” she asked.

“Most of it,” Michael said. “We're still negotiating the pancake schedule.”

Margaret looked at Emily. “A sensible priority.”

Elizabeth joined them, drying her hands. “Your mother and I managed a great deal with much less planning.”

“Your mother once got us lost on a school trip,” Margaret said.

“You were holding the map upside down.”

“It was a menu,” James called from the living room.

The adults burst into laughter. Emily looked at Michael, and he looked back at her. Their families had become a kind of landscape: familiar, imperfect, full of stories that began long before they did.

Later, when Emily was home, she placed Michael's calendar beside her writing notebook. She added the program's dates to her own calendar and circled the final presentation.

Then she opened a fresh page and wrote:

*The future is not a place we arrive at all at once. Sometimes it is a paper map folded into someone's jacket, with train times in the margins and a promise to meet at the station.*

She paused, then added:

*Sometimes love is not asking someone to stay. It is learning how to find one another on the way.*

She closed the notebook.

Across the river, Michael was beginning to prepare for a summer that would challenge him. Emily was beginning to understand that the life they were building would not be made only of grand moments. It would be made of plans, missed trains, late messages, small reconciliations, and the decision to keep showing up.

The map was not a guarantee.

It was a beginning.
