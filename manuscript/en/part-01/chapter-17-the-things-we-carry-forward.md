# Chapter 17: The Things We Carry Forward

Senior year began with a timetable, a new set of expectations, and the discovery that the school had replaced the old library chairs with furniture that appeared to have been designed by someone who disliked sitting.

Emily and Sarah claimed a table near the windows. Michael's engineering elective met in the building across the courtyard, so he sometimes appeared between classes with a notebook under his arm and a question about lunch.

The year moved quickly. Applications became essays, essays became deadlines, and weekends filled with visits to campuses. Emily considered literature and communications programs. Michael leaned toward civil engineering, though he kept a list of architecture courses and public-design electives.

They visited a university together on a bright October afternoon. The campus was larger than either expected, with old stone buildings, a modern library, and a broad lawn scattered with students.

Emily liked the library immediately. Michael liked the engineering building, especially a workshop where students were assembling a small pedestrian bridge.

“You look at this place as if you could move in today,” Emily said.

“I could.”

“You haven't even seen the cafeteria.”

“I have priorities.”

She smiled. “I like the library.”

“Of course you do.”

They spent the afternoon attending information sessions. Emily asked about writing seminars and student publications. Michael asked about design studios, field placements, and the practical work students completed outside the classroom.

On the train home, they compared notes.

“I think I could be happy there,” Emily said.

“I think I could too.”

The possibility hung between them. They did not yet know whether they would choose the same university, different universities, or programs that led them in separate directions. For the first time, their shared future had several plausible versions.

That evening, Emily told Sarah about the visit.

“Would you choose a school because Michael might go there?” Sarah asked.

Emily took a moment. “I would consider it. But I don't want it to be the only reason.”

“Good.”

“I want a place where I can write. Where I can meet people who care about the same things. Where I can become better.”

“And if he chooses somewhere else?”

“Then we'll have to decide what works for both of us.”

Sarah nodded. “That's a grown-up answer.”

“I hate it.”

“Most useful answers are inconvenient.”

At the Reed house, Michael was having a similar conversation with Ashley.

“You're not going to choose a university based on Emily's application, are you?” Ashley asked.

“I'm not choosing anything yet.”

“That's not an answer.”

Michael closed his laptop. “I want to be near her. I also want the strongest program I can get into. Those aren't necessarily opposites.”

Ashley considered him. “Just make sure you don't turn love into a reason to avoid making your own decisions.”

“Since when did you become an advice columnist?”

“Since you started asking questions with your face.”

He threw a cushion at her. She caught it.

The families met for dinner the following Sunday. Margaret and Elizabeth had planned a meal around the old recipes they had collected over the years. The kitchen was full of chopping boards, handwritten notes, and arguments about whether a certain sauce needed more lemon.

Emily helped Margaret prepare the vegetables. Elizabeth was telling David about a school event from their youth, and James was trying to convince Ashley that his record collection contained an album she would enjoy.

“Your father has been trying to convince people of that for twenty years,” Elizabeth said.

“It is a masterpiece,” James replied.

“It is a very long song about a train.”

“A metaphorical train.”

“Still a train.”

Margaret laughed so hard she had to put down the spoon.

Emily watched the two women. They had known each other long before the children, long before the dinners and school events and the complicated future now waiting for them. Their friendship had survived distance, changing jobs, marriages, and the years in which raising children left little time for anything else.

“Do you ever wish you could go back?” Emily asked Margaret.

Margaret glanced at her. “To when?”

“To when you and Mom were young.”

Margaret thought about it. “Sometimes I miss the people we were. But I don't want to exchange the people we became.”

Elizabeth nodded. “There are things I would do differently. But I wouldn't trade the life that came after.”

“Even the scooter?” Ashley asked.

“Especially the scooter,” Margaret said. “It taught me never to trust your mother with directions.”

Elizabeth flicked a dish towel at her.

The laughter moved through the kitchen, familiar and bright. Emily understood that the women were not simply remembering the past. They were carrying it forward, making it available to the people who came after them.

After dinner, Michael found Emily in the hallway looking at a framed photograph of the two families from a summer picnic.

“What's that expression?” he asked.

“I'm thinking about how our families became one another's history.”

He stood beside her. “That's a lot of history.”

“And a lot of embarrassing stories.”

“Mostly about our mothers.”

“And you.”

“I have done nothing.”

“You once tried to build a skateboard ramp from a garden shelf.”

“It was an experiment.”

“It was a disaster.”

“It was a successful demonstration of structural failure.”

She laughed. He looked pleased with himself.

Then his expression grew thoughtful. “I got an interview invitation from the engineering school.”

Emily turned toward him. “Michael, that's wonderful.”

“It's next month.”

“I'm proud of you.”

He took her hand. “I also heard back from the university we visited. They want me to attend an open day.”

“That's two good things.”

“It's beginning to feel real.”

“It is real.”

He nodded. “Have you heard from the writing program?”

“Not yet. They asked for another sample.”

“Then we'll celebrate when it comes.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“Then we'll find something else to celebrate.”

She smiled. “You have become very good at that.”

“At what?”

“Making room for more than one outcome.”

He looked at the photograph. “I learned from someone.”

For a while they stood together, looking at the people in the picture: Margaret with her arm around Elizabeth, David holding a paper plate, James squinting into the sun, Ashley laughing, and Michael standing beside Emily with a hand resting lightly on her shoulder.

The photograph was not perfect. Someone had moved. The light was too bright. The edge of the frame cut off half a picnic table.

But it held a day that had mattered.

Emily thought about her essay, the writing competition, and the way a small detail could open a larger door. Perhaps a family was like that too. Not a perfect arrangement, but a collection of moments that became meaningful because people kept choosing to return.

She rested her head against Michael's shoulder.

“Whatever happens with university,” she said, “I want us to keep making memories that aren't just photographs.”

“We will.”

“Real ones.”

He turned and kissed her hair. “Especially those.”

Later, Emily added the picnic photograph to the folder she was assembling for her writing portfolio. She did not use it as an illustration. She simply wanted to remember the people who had made the room larger.

The future would ask them to carry many things: ambitions, disappointments, new friendships, and the distance between places. They could not carry everything.

But they could carry forward the parts that taught them how to love.
