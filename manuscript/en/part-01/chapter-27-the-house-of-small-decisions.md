# Chapter 27: The House of Small Decisions

The apartment search began with a list of requirements and ended with a disagreement about a kitchen window.

Emily wanted a place with enough light for reading and writing. Michael wanted a reliable commute and a building that did not appear to be held together by optimistic paint. They both wanted two bedrooms if they could afford them, though one would have to become a study.

Their budget was careful. They had saved, compared salaries, and spoken honestly about what they could manage without depending on future promotions or imagined bonuses.

The first apartment was too dark. The second had a beautiful kitchen but a bedroom that opened directly onto a noisy street. The third was technically close to both workplaces but had a heating system that sounded like a machine trying to escape.

Then they visited a modest apartment in a quiet neighbourhood, a short walk from a train station. It had a small balcony, two bedrooms, and a kitchen window that looked toward a row of trees.

Emily stood in the living room and watched the afternoon light move across the floor.

“I can imagine writing here,” she said.

Michael tested the window latch.

“It's solid,” he announced.

“That's your romantic assessment?”

“It is a very important window.”

She laughed.

The landlord explained the lease, the maintenance arrangements, and the deposit. Michael asked sensible questions. Emily checked the distance to the nearest bookshop. They left without making a decision.

At a café nearby, they compared notes.

“I like it,” Emily said.

“So do I.”

“Can we afford it?”

Michael opened the spreadsheet on his phone. They reviewed rent, utilities, transport, food, savings, and the costs they would need to cover before the wedding.

“If we keep the furniture simple and don't buy everything at once,” he said, “we can manage.”

“I don't want us to spend every spare dollar just to make the apartment look finished.”

“Neither do I.”

“Let's live in it first.”

He smiled. “That's sensible.”

“I'm capable of being sensible.”

“I have evidence.”

They applied the next day and received approval before the weekend.

The news reached both families quickly. Margaret offered kitchen equipment. Elizabeth had boxes of linens. James volunteered to help with shelves. David offered a set of tools. Ashley insisted that the apartment needed a proper mirror. Sarah offered to organize a housewarming. Sophia wanted to help choose curtains. Olivia suggested a small bookshelf for the living room.

Madison sent a list of affordable furniture shops and a note reminding them to check delivery costs.

Emily looked at the messages and laughed.

“We have become a community project,” she said.

Michael kissed her cheek. “A well-supported one.”

Moving day was chaotic. The lift stopped once, a box of books split open, and Ashley discovered that the mirror she had brought was too large for the hallway.

“It's not too large,” she insisted. “The hallway is too narrow.”

“That's a useful distinction,” James said.

Margaret and Elizabeth arrived with food. David assembled a table. Michael and James installed shelves while Emily and Sarah unpacked the kitchen. Sophia arranged cushions and then rearranged them. Olivia labeled boxes with a system that only she understood.

By late afternoon, the apartment had begun to look lived in. The books were on shelves. The kitchen contained more utensils than the couple could identify. A small vase of flowers stood on the table.

Emily stepped onto the balcony. Michael joined her, carrying two glasses of water.

“It's ours,” she said.

“It is.”

“That's a strange feeling.”

“Good strange?”

“Very.”

They looked down at the street. A child was riding a scooter, and someone was walking a dog. The neighbourhood carried on around them, unaware that a new chapter had begun in the small apartment above.

That evening, after everyone left, Emily and Michael sat on the floor among the remaining boxes and ate takeaway food.

“We should have bought plates,” Emily said.

“We have plates.”

“We have two plates.”

“That's enough for two people.”

“What if we invite someone?”

“We can ask them to bring their own.”

She laughed.

They ate from the cartons, leaning against the sofa they had not yet assembled.

“Are you happy?” he asked.

Emily looked around. The apartment was not perfect. The curtains were temporary, the bedroom still contained unopened boxes, and the kitchen window needed a second latch. But the light was warm, and Michael was beside her.

“Yes,” she said. “I'm happy.”

He reached for her hand.

“Me too.”

They spent the next hour making a list of what remained to be done. A desk for Emily. A lamp for the study. Better storage in the hallway. A rug. A proper set of plates. They agreed not to buy anything until they had lived with the space for a few weeks.

Before bed, Emily placed the school photograph of herself and Michael on the bookshelf. Beside it, she set the picture from their first anniversary walk.

Two beginnings, one beside the other.

Michael stood behind her and wrapped his arms around her waist.

“Do you remember the first time I came to your house?” he asked.

“You were carrying a notebook and pretending you weren't nervous.”

“I was not pretending.”

“You were.”

He kissed her shoulder. “Do you remember the first time you came to mine?”

“Your mother made too much food.”

“She still does.”

“And Ashley showed me your childhood photographs.”

“She should have been stopped.”

Emily laughed. “I'm glad she wasn't.”

He turned her gently toward him. “I like that this place will have its own memories.”

“It already does.”

“Which ones?”

“The box that broke. The mirror that didn't fit. The two plates. The very important window.”

He smiled. “A strong foundation.”

She kissed him.

Later, when the lights were off, the apartment made unfamiliar settling sounds. Emily listened to them and realized that home did not arrive fully furnished. It grew from small decisions: where to put the books, how to divide the chores, when to ask for help, and how to laugh when the plan failed.

The house was not finished.

Neither were they.

That was the point.
