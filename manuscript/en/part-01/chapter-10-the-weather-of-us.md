# Chapter 10: The Weather of Us

The first warm Saturday of April arrived with the confidence of someone who had been invited and intended to stay.

Emily noticed it before she noticed anything else: the open windows in the Bennett kitchen, the smell of wet earth rising from the garden, and her mother humming while she arranged tulips in a glass vase. The winter coats had not yet been put away, but the house had begun to behave as if spring were already a promise.

Her phone vibrated beside her notebook.

**Michael:** The weather report says sunshine.  
**Emily:** The weather report also said rain yesterday.  
**Michael:** It was emotionally raining.  
**Emily:** That is not a meteorological category.  
**Michael:** It is in New Jersey.

She smiled down at the screen.

**Michael:** Walk by the river? Two o'clock?  
**Emily:** Are you asking me on a date or inviting me to inspect water?  
**Michael:** I can do both. I contain multitudes.

She typed yes, deleted it, then typed it again with a small addition.

**Emily:** Yes. But wear a jacket. You always underestimate the wind.

His reply came almost immediately.

**Michael:** I have been warned by the leading authority on my poor decisions.

At two, he was waiting near the station with two coffees and a paper bag folded at the top. He wore the navy jacket Emily had once told him made him look older than he was. His hair had been pushed sideways by the wind, and he was trying to flatten it with the palm of his hand.

“You look like you argued with a weather system,” she said.

“I negotiated.”

“And lost?”

“On a technicality.”

He handed her a coffee. The paper bag contained a cinnamon roll, still faintly warm.

“You remembered,” she said.

“You told me once that cinnamon rolls improve almost any difficult day.”

“I said they improve Mondays.”

“Today is Saturday. I took a risk.”

They started along the river path. The Hudson moved beneath a pale sky, carrying the reflection of buildings in long, broken lines. On the opposite bank, the city seemed close enough to touch and far enough away to belong to another life.

For a while they talked about ordinary things: a teacher's impossible deadline, Sarah's latest plan to persuade the school newspaper to print a photography supplement, and Michael's argument with a geometry problem that he insisted had been designed out of personal spite.

Then Emily asked, “Have you decided about the summer program?”

Michael's expression changed—not dramatically, but enough for her to notice. He looked ahead, turning the coffee cup between his hands.

“The engineering program in the city?”

“You said you were applying.”

“I am. I haven't heard back.”

“You'll get in.”

“You don't know that.”

“I know how you work.”

He glanced at her. “That isn't the same thing.”

“No. It's better.”

He gave a quiet laugh, but it faded quickly. “If I get in, it means a longer commute. More time away. And if I don't, I'll have spent three months pretending I didn't want it.”

Emily stopped beside the railing. “You should want it. You should apply for every opportunity that makes you curious.”

“What if it changes things?”

“Things are allowed to change.”

He studied her face as though he were trying to decide whether she believed her own answer.

She did, mostly. The smaller part of her—the part that counted the minutes between his messages and remembered every time he had walked her to the station—wanted to ask him not to go anywhere. She did not want that part making decisions for either of them.

“I don't want you to make yourself smaller so I can feel comfortable,” she said.

Michael's shoulders eased. “I wasn't planning to.”

“Good.”

“I might, however, ask you to help me choose a decent shirt for the interview.”

“That is a completely different matter. I have standards.”

“I was hoping you did.”

They walked again. A cyclist passed too close, and Michael moved instinctively to the outside of the path, putting himself between Emily and the bicycle. She caught his sleeve.

“I can survive a bicycle.”

“I know.”

“Then why do you always do that?”

He looked genuinely puzzled. “Do what?”

“Move to the outside. Carry the heavier bag. Check whether I've got my gloves. You behave as if the world is full of small emergencies.”

“Not the world,” he said. “Just the parts where you're standing.”

She looked away before he could see how much the sentence had affected her.

At the next bench, they shared the cinnamon roll. Michael broke it unevenly and tried to give her the larger half. Emily switched the pieces while he was looking at the river.

“That's cheating,” he said.

“That's justice.”

“Your legal system is corrupt.”

“Only when necessary.”

A little girl nearby was trying to teach her grandfather how to skip stones. Each attempt landed with a flat splash. Michael watched them for a moment, then picked up a smooth stone and demonstrated. It skipped three times.

The girl stared at him as if he had performed a magic trick.

“Again!” she demanded.

He gave her the stone. “Your turn.”

Her next attempt skipped once. She threw both hands into the air.

Michael applauded with complete seriousness.

Emily watched him. There was something about the way he treated a child's small victory as if it mattered that made her chest tighten. He never seemed to understand that kindness could be more revealing than grand gestures.

On the train back, they sat shoulder to shoulder. Michael opened his notebook to show her a rough sketch for a pedestrian bridge he had been working on. The lines were careful, but the margins were crowded with tiny notes.

“You draw bridges when you're nervous,” Emily said.

“I draw bridges when I'm thinking.”

“You draw them more when you're nervous.”

He considered that. “Maybe bridges are what I think about when I don't know how to say something.”

“What do you need to say?”

He closed the notebook gently. “That I want you to come to the program's final presentation if I get accepted. And if I don't, I want you to come with me for pancakes so I can complain about the selection committee.”

“I can commit to both.”

“That's generous.”

“I'm known for my generosity.”

He reached for her hand. Their fingers settled together as naturally as if they had been waiting for the train to stop moving.

At the station, he walked her to the entrance. The afternoon had cooled, and the wind had returned with the stubbornness of an old argument.

“Text me when you're home,” he said.

“You'll know. You'll receive a formal report.”

“I expect charts.”

“Obviously.”

He hesitated, then brushed a loose strand of hair away from her cheek. The gesture was so gentle that it made the busy station seem briefly quiet.

“I'm glad you said yes today,” he said.

“To the walk?”

“To the possibility of things changing without us having to be afraid of them.”

Emily squeezed his hand. “We can learn how.”

He kissed her forehead, then stepped back so she could go inside.

That evening, Sarah called while Emily was sorting photographs from the walk.

“You have that expression,” Sarah announced.

“What expression?”

“The one where you are pretending to organize pictures but actually looking at the same photograph of Michael for eleven minutes.”

“I am not.”

“Then tell me what the photograph contains.”

Emily looked at the screen. Michael was laughing at something outside the frame, his eyes narrowed against the sun. “A boy who thinks he can negotiate with the weather.”

Sarah made a thoughtful noise. “Serious condition. Is it treatable?”

“Probably not.”

“Then I recommend continued exposure.”

Emily laughed and saved the photograph.

Later, she wrote in the notebook she had begun using for the writing competition. She did not write about Michael as a perfect person. She wrote about the way he tried to hide worry behind jokes, the way he could be stubborn when he cared, and how he had begun to understand that loving someone did not mean keeping them still.

At the bottom of the page she wrote:

*Perhaps growing up is learning that love is not a promise that nothing will change. It is the decision to keep meeting one another honestly as the seasons do.*

She closed the notebook.

Outside, the wind moved through the new leaves. Somewhere across the river, Michael was probably drawing another bridge.

For the first time, the distance between their homes did not feel like a test. It felt like a stretch of water they already knew how to cross.
