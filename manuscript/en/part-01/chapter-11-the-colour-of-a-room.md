# Chapter 11: The Colour of a Room

The Bennett house had never been particularly quiet, but on the afternoon of Elizabeth's birthday it became almost impossible to hear a complete sentence.

Sarah was in the dining room arguing with a roll of ribbon. Sophia had taken possession of the kitchen counter and was arranging cupcakes by colour. Olivia, who had arrived carrying a box of carefully wrapped books, was explaining to James Bennett why the labels on his record collection were not arranged according to any recognized system.

“They are arranged according to the order in which I bought them,” James said.

“That is a chronology, not a system,” Olivia replied.

“It is a system with a personal history.”

“An unreliable one.”

Emily watched them from the doorway. “Dad, you're losing.”

“I am being outnumbered.”

“You invited them.”

“I was not informed that they would bring evidence.”

Elizabeth came through the hall with a tray of glasses. “If everyone could save their arguments until after the cake, I would be grateful.”

“No promises,” Olivia said.

The birthday gathering was meant to be small. Elizabeth had insisted on it. But a small gathering in the Bennett family tended to expand through friendship, food, and the inability of anyone to leave without being offered something to take home.

Margaret Reed arrived with Ashley just before five. Margaret carried a dish covered in a checked cloth and wore the bright red scarf that Elizabeth had given her years earlier. Ashley followed with a gift bag and a look of amused resignation.

“I was told this was a quiet birthday,” Ashley said.

“It was,” Emily replied. “Then Sarah arrived.”

Sarah raised the ribbon in protest. “This ribbon attacked me first.”

Margaret laughed, and the room seemed to warm around the sound. She had a way of entering a gathering that made everyone feel expected. Even James, who claimed not to enjoy parties, found himself smiling when she began telling Elizabeth about a disastrous school trip from their teenage years.

“You cannot tell that story again,” Elizabeth warned.

“I absolutely can,” Margaret said. “You were the one who told the bus driver that the map was upside down.”

“It was upside down.”

“It was a menu.”

Ashley leaned toward Emily. “They have been telling this story for years. The details change every time.”

“That's how legends work,” Emily whispered.

Michael arrived last, carrying a bouquet of pale pink tulips. He found Emily near the staircase and held them out.

“For your mother,” he said. “I was told she likes these.”

“She does. You remembered.”

“I listen occasionally.”

“Only occasionally?”

“I don't want to create unrealistic expectations.”

Emily accepted the flowers. Their hands touched, and he smiled at her in the small private way that had become familiar: a smile that belonged to the space between them, even in a crowded room.

“You look nice,” he said.

She glanced down at her simple blue dress. “I nearly changed.”

“I'm glad you didn't.”

“Why?”

“Because I like this colour on you.”

He said it without performance, then went to greet her father. Emily watched him shake James's hand and accept a mock-serious warning about returning his daughter at a reasonable hour.

The evening unfolded in overlapping conversations. Olivia and Sophia discovered that they both loved old films, though for entirely different reasons. Sophia admired the costumes; Olivia wanted to discuss the cinematography. Sarah took photographs, then complained that everyone moved just as she pressed the shutter. Ashley and Michael argued about which of them had been the more difficult child. Margaret supplied evidence against both.

Madison arrived with her mother a little later than expected. She wore a tailored cream jacket and carried a gift wrapped in silver paper. She greeted Emily warmly, though there was a carefulness in her expression that Emily had learned not to mistake for dislike.

“Happy birthday to your mother,” Madison said. “I hope I'm not too late.”

“Not at all. Come in.”

Madison handed Elizabeth the gift and joined the group near the windows. She was good at entering conversations. She remembered names, asked questions, and could make a person feel interesting without appearing to work at it. Emily sometimes found that skill intimidating. She also knew it was a skill, not a crime.

When Michael came over, Madison asked him about the engineering program.

“I heard you applied,” she said. “My cousin did it last year. He said the design studio was excellent.”

“That's what I'm hoping for.”

“You should show them your bridge model. It would be perfect for the exhibition.”

Michael glanced at Emily, then back at Madison. “If I get accepted, maybe.”

“You will,” Madison said. “You always seem to know what you're doing.”

He laughed. “That is an extremely generous misunderstanding.”

Madison smiled. “I don't think it's a misunderstanding.”

Emily felt the old, familiar pinch—not a sharp jealousy, but the uncomfortable awareness that other people could see qualities in Michael too. She disliked the feeling because it made her want to become someone she was not.

Sophia appeared at her elbow with two cupcakes.

“Here,” she said. “Eat something before you start mentally competing with the curtains.”

Emily blinked. “I wasn't.”

“Of course not. You were simply studying the room with the intensity of a museum curator.”

Emily laughed despite herself. “You are impossible.”

“I'm observant. There is a difference.”

Sophia handed her a cupcake and nodded toward Madison. “She's trying to impress him. That doesn't mean she's trying to take anything from you.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Emily looked at her. Sophia's tone was lighter than the words, but not careless.

“I don't want to become the sort of person who treats every woman as a threat,” Emily said.

“Then don't. Madison can be ambitious and still be a person. You can feel insecure and still be generous. Both things can be true.”

Emily studied her friend. “When did you become wise?”

“After years of being underestimated. It gives you time to think.”

Across the room, Michael was helping James move a chair. He looked up and caught Emily's eye. He did not make a show of coming to her. He simply gave her a small smile, then returned to the task.

The gesture settled something inside her. He did not need to prove his affection by dismissing everyone else. Nor did she need to measure it by how many people admired him.

After dinner, Elizabeth opened her gifts. The tulips went into a vase at the centre of the table. Margaret's dish was declared too delicious to be allowed to leave the house. Olivia's books made Elizabeth laugh because she had once mentioned wanting to read them and forgotten the titles. From Emily, she received a framed photograph: Elizabeth and Margaret standing together at a seaside promenade years earlier, both laughing, their hair blown across their faces.

Elizabeth held it for a long time.

“I thought you might like a picture from before we all became sensible,” Emily said.

Margaret leaned over her shoulder. “I was never sensible.”

“You once tried to drive a borrowed scooter through a hotel lobby,” Elizabeth said.

“It was raining.”

“Inside?”

“It was a complicated day.”

Everyone laughed. Even James, who had heard the story before, laughed as if it were new.

Later, while the guests were gathering coats, Margaret found Emily in the kitchen.

“You have a good heart,” she said.

Emily looked up from the plates. “Thank you.”

“And you are allowed to have difficult feelings. Just don't let them write your decisions for you.”

Emily glanced toward the living room, where Michael was helping Ashley find her gloves. “I don't want to lose what we have.”

“Then take care of it. Not by holding it too tightly. By being honest.”

Margaret touched her shoulder and returned to the others.

When the house had finally quieted, Emily stepped outside with Michael. The night air smelled of damp grass and the faint smoke of a neighbour's fireplace.

“Your mother had a wonderful birthday,” he said.

“She did. Your mother helped.”

“Our mothers are a dangerous combination.”

“They have been a dangerous combination since before we were born.”

He laughed. “I heard the scooter story again.”

“Which version?”

“The one involving a hotel lobby, a borrowed scooter, and a security guard named Frank.”

“That version is mostly true.”

“Mostly?”

“The scooter was not borrowed.”

Michael shook his head. “I am beginning to understand where you get your talent for selective storytelling.”

She nudged him with her shoulder. “You like my stories.”

“I like the person telling them.”

He said it simply. Emily leaned against him, and he put an arm around her. Through the window they could see the framed photograph on the dining-room table: two young women laughing at a seaside promenade, long before either could imagine the families that would grow around them.

“Do you think we'll be like them?” Emily asked.

“Like our mothers?”

“Still laughing together years from now. Still telling the same stories.”

“I hope so.”

“What if we become boring?”

“Then we'll become boring together. We can organize our records by purchase date and argue about it.”

“That's your idea of romance?”

“I have several ideas. That was the affordable one.”

She laughed, and he kissed her temple.

When he left, Emily went back inside and found her mother in the dining room, carefully placing the framed photograph on the shelf.

“Keep that one somewhere you can see it,” Emily said.

“I will.” Elizabeth smiled. “It reminds me that the best parts of life often begin before we know what they will become.”

Emily looked at the photograph again. She thought of friendships that survived years, of families that became connected by accident and choice, and of the many ways a room could change when the right people entered it.

She had once thought romance was a story about two people.

Now she was beginning to understand that love also made a room larger.
