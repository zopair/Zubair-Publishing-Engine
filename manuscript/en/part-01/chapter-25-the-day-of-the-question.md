# Chapter 25: The Day of the Question

Michael spent three weeks trying to choose a day.

He considered the date of their first meeting, the anniversary of their first proper date, and the first Saturday of spring. Each option seemed either too obvious or too ordinary. Then he realized that he was searching for a perfect day when what mattered was the life they had already shared.

He chose a Sunday in May.

He asked Sarah for help because Sarah knew Emily's habits better than anyone outside the family. He asked Ashley because she had strong opinions about everything, including the correct way to wrap a small box. He asked Margaret and Elizabeth because he wanted the two women who had watched their story grow to be part of the day.

The plan was simple. A walk by the river, a picnic in the place where they had once shared a cinnamon roll, and dinner with both families afterward. No audience, no public spectacle, no hidden photographer waiting behind a tree.

Sarah approved.

“Good,” she said. “She would hate a crowd.”

“I know.”

“And don't make a speech that sounds like a university application.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“You were absolutely planning to.”

Ashley offered to help choose the ring. Michael showed her three designs, and she rejected two immediately.

“This one,” she said, pointing to a simple gold band with a small oval stone.

“Why?”

“Because she wears delicate jewellery. She likes things that feel personal, not things that announce themselves from across a room.”

Michael studied the ring again. “That's what I thought.”

“Then why did you ask me?”

“To confirm.”

Ashley gave him a satisfied nod. “Excellent. I have contributed.”

The morning of the proposal, Michael woke before his alarm. He made coffee, checked the weather, and checked the ring box three times. Margaret found him standing in the kitchen, looking at the small box as if it might change shape.

“Have you eaten?” she asked.

“No.”

“Then start there. You cannot ask an important question on an empty stomach.”

He ate toast because his mother watched him until he did.

Emily, meanwhile, believed they were going for their usual Sunday walk. She wore a cream sweater, dark jeans, and the small gold earrings Margaret had given her for her birthday. She had no idea that Sarah had spent the previous evening making sure the picnic basket was ready.

Michael met her outside the station.

“You look nervous,” she said.

“I'm thinking.”

“That's usually dangerous.”

“Today it may be.”

She laughed and took his hand.

They walked along the river path, passing the bench where they had once shared their first cinnamon roll. Michael had arranged for a small picnic to be set up farther along, beneath a tree whose branches reached toward the water. The blanket was blue. There were strawberries, sandwiches, two bottles of sparkling lemonade, and a paper bag from their favourite bakery.

Emily stopped.

“You planned this?”

“I thought we could make a day of it.”

She looked at the food, then at him. “You remembered everything.”

“I had help.”

“From whom?”

“People who are very difficult to keep quiet.”

She smiled and sat beside him.

They ate slowly. Michael told her about a project meeting that had gone wrong, and Emily described a manuscript she was helping edit. They discussed the apartment they might rent after the summer, the possibility of adopting a dog one day, and whether either of them could be trusted to keep a plant alive.

“You can build a bridge,” Emily said, “but you cannot remember to water a basil plant.”

“Basil is temperamental.”

“It's a plant.”

“Exactly. No clear instructions.”

She laughed. The sound made him feel both calm and terrified.

After lunch, they walked closer to the water. The afternoon light lay across the river in long silver bands. Michael stopped near the railing.

“Emily,” he said.

She turned toward him. His expression was different now, serious but not uncertain.

“What is it?”

He took both her hands.

“I have been thinking about the life we've built so far. The school library, the train rides, the missed calls, the arguments about pastries, the days when we were proud of each other, and the days when we had to learn how to listen better.”

She smiled, her eyes already shining.

“I don't want to promise you that everything will always be easy,” he continued. “I can't promise that we won't change or that we will always know what to do. But I can promise to keep showing up. To make room for your work and your dreams. To tell you the truth. To laugh with you when we can, and to sit beside you when we can't.”

He released one hand and took the small box from his pocket.

“I want to keep building this life with you. Emily Bennett, will you marry me?”

For a moment she could not speak. She looked at the ring, then at Michael, whose eyes were bright with the same emotion she felt rising through her.

“Yes,” she whispered.

He searched her face. “Yes?”

She laughed through tears. “Yes, Michael. Of course.”

He placed the ring on her finger. It fit.

Then she pulled him into an embrace, and he held her as if the world had become both enormous and very still.

“You said yes,” he murmured.

“I did.”

“I was hoping you would.”

“You were?”

“I had a backup plan involving several more pastries.”

She laughed against his shoulder.

He kissed her. It was not a perfect cinematic moment. The wind caught her hair, the picnic blanket shifted, and a passing cyclist called out an apology for ringing his bell too late. But it was theirs.

When they finally stepped apart, Emily held up her hand.

“It's beautiful.”

“I hoped you would like it.”

“I love it.”

He kissed her fingers. “I love you.”

“I love you too.”

They sat together until the sun began to lower. Emily kept looking at the ring, then at Michael, as if she needed to confirm that both were real.

When they reached the station, Sarah was waiting at the far end of the platform. She tried to look casual and failed.

Emily held up her hand.

Sarah made a sound that was half laugh and half cry, then rushed forward to hug her.

“You said yes!”

“I did.”

“I knew you would.”

“You did not. You threatened to interrogate me if I said no.”

“That's because I had prepared questions.”

Ashley appeared behind her, holding a small bouquet. “Congratulations. I would like it recorded that I chose the ring.”

“You did,” Michael said.

“And I was right.”

“You were.”

The families gathered at the Bennett house that evening. Margaret cried when she saw the ring. Elizabeth cried when she hugged Emily. David shook Michael's hand and then pulled him into an embrace. James stood quietly for a moment before he said, “Welcome to the family, officially.”

Michael's eyes softened. “Thank you.”

The dinner was loud, full of stories, questions, and teasing. Margaret insisted on telling the story of the first time Emily had visited the Reed house. Elizabeth corrected several details. Ashley announced that she expected to be consulted about the wedding playlist. Sarah demanded a role in the planning. Sophia offered to help with dresses and styling. Olivia suggested a reading for the ceremony. Madison sent a message of congratulations and offered to help with the charity arrangements if they wanted a smaller community celebration.

Emily looked around the room and felt the size of the life that had gathered around her.

Later, when the guests had gone, she stood with Michael in the garden.

“Are you happy?” he asked.

She looked at the ring again. “I'm happier than I know how to explain.”

He took her hand. “Then we can take our time explaining it.”

She rested her head against his shoulder.

The question had been asked. The answer had been given.

The life ahead was still waiting to be built, one ordinary day at a time.
