# Chapter 1 — The Seat by the Window

The first time Emily Bennett noticed Michael Reed, he was arguing with a vending machine.

Not quietly, either.

He stood in the school hallway, one hand resting against the machine, the other holding a dollar bill that had apparently been rejected for the third time.

“I'm beginning to think this machine has something personal against me,” he said.

Emily slowed her steps.

The machine hummed.

Michael inserted the bill again.

It spat it back.

He stared at it.

“Fine. Keep your secrets.”

Emily laughed before she could stop herself.

Michael turned.

For a second, neither of them spoke.

Then he smiled.

It was not the kind of smile that tried to impress anyone. It arrived naturally, as though he had been waiting for a reason to smile and had finally found one.

“Something funny?” he asked.

“I'm just wondering how you managed to lose an argument with a machine.”

“I didn't lose.”

“You're standing here negotiating with a vending machine.”

“That's called diplomacy.”

Emily raised an eyebrow.

“Then I hope you're better at international relations.”

Michael laughed.

It was the first sound she would remember about him years later—not his voice saying her name, not his promises, not even the words he would eventually whisper on the night they became engaged.

Just that laugh.

Uncomplicated. Warm. Entirely unaware of how much it would one day mean to her.

“My name's Michael,” he said, offering his hand.

“Emily.”

“I know.”

She looked at him suspiciously.

“You know?”

He pointed toward the classroom door behind her.

“Your name is on your notebook. You dropped it when you were walking in.”

Emily glanced down.

Her notebook was indeed missing.

Michael reached behind him and picked it up from the floor beside the vending machine.

She took it.

“Thank you.”

“You're welcome. And for the record, I was going to win that argument.”

“Of course you were.”

He stepped aside, allowing her to pass.

“See you around, Emily.”

She walked away without looking back.

At least, not until she reached the corner.

Then she glanced over her shoulder.

He was still trying to convince the vending machine to release his snack.

She smiled to herself.

She had no idea that an ordinary school morning had just introduced her to the person who would become the greatest love of her life.

***

Their school stood in a quiet neighborhood outside New York City, where the mornings smelled of wet pavement, coffee, and the changing seasons.

Emily had grown up in Westchester County, in a house where the kitchen was always warm, the windows were usually open, and her mother believed that almost every problem could be improved with a cup of tea and a conversation.

Her father, James, was a practical man. He believed in punctuality, responsibility, and checking the weather before leaving the house.

Her mother, Elizabeth, believed in spontaneous road trips, dancing while cooking, and occasionally buying things simply because they were beautiful.

Emily had inherited a little of both.

She was thoughtful, observant, and quietly romantic. She noticed details other people overlooked: the color of the sky before rain, the way someone looked when they were trying not to cry, and whether a friend had gone unusually quiet.

Michael, on the other hand, lived across the Hudson River in New Jersey.

His family was louder.

Not in an unpleasant way. Their house simply never seemed to run out of conversation.

His mother, Mrs. Reed, had a laugh that could fill an entire room. She was known among her friends for turning minor inconveniences into dramatic performances.

His father, David, was calmer, with a dry sense of humor that often appeared when everyone else had run out of things to say.

Michael had inherited his mother's ability to make people laugh and his father's habit of pretending he was not amused.

He was also, Emily would later discover, terrible at remembering where he had put his keys.

***

They met again two days later.

This time, there was no vending machine involved.

Emily entered English literature class and found that most of the seats were already taken.

Only one remained beside the window.

Michael was sitting there.

He looked up as she approached.

“Diplomacy,” he said, moving his backpack off the empty chair.

Emily stopped.

“You're following me now?”

“I'm beginning to think you're following me.”

She placed her books on the desk.

“That's an impressive level of confidence.”

“Thank you. I've been practicing.”

She sat down.

Outside, the trees moved gently in the wind. A few students were still talking while their teacher arranged papers at the front of the room.

Michael leaned slightly toward her.

“Did you get your notebook home safely?”

“Yes.”

“Good. I was worried.”

“About the notebook?”

“About my reputation. I don't usually rescue people twice in one week.”

Emily shook her head, trying not to smile.

Their teacher began the lesson.

They were asked to read a short passage from a novel and discuss what made a relationship meaningful.

Michael wrote something in the margin of his notebook.

Emily glanced at it.

*Someone who remembers the small things.*

She looked at him.

He shrugged.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You're looking at my notes.”

“Your handwriting is distracting.”

“That's the nicest criticism I've ever received.”

She returned to her book.

But she remembered the sentence.

Someone who remembers the small things.

She did not know why it stayed with her.

Perhaps because she had always believed that love was made of grand gestures: flowers, beautiful dinners, unexpected trips, and promises spoken beneath perfect skies.

That morning, sitting beside a boy she barely knew, she began to suspect that it might be something much smaller.

A notebook picked up from the floor.

A seat saved beside a window.

A person who noticed.

***

At lunch, Emily found herself sitting with Sarah Brooks, her closest friend at school.

Sarah was already halfway through a sandwich and telling a story with the urgency of someone reporting a national emergency.

“I'm telling you, he looked directly at me.”

“Who?”

“Ethan. From chemistry.”

“Maybe he was looking at the clock behind you.”

Sarah stopped chewing.

“Emily. I need you to be supportive.”

“I am supportive.”

“No. You're being accurate. That's much worse.”

Emily laughed.

Sarah leaned forward.

“Now, tell me why you're smiling.”

“I'm not.”

“You are.”

“I'm just having a good day.”

Sarah narrowed her eyes.

“Interesting.”

“What's interesting?”

“You've been having a good day since literature class.”

Emily reached for her drink.

Sarah followed her gaze across the cafeteria.

Michael was sitting with two boys near the windows. He was telling them something, moving his hands as he spoke. One of them burst into laughter.

Sarah looked back at Emily.

“Oh.”

Emily frowned.

“What does that mean?”

“It means absolutely nothing.”

“Sarah.”

“I'm simply observing.”

“Observe your lunch.”

Sarah grinned.

“I will. But I have excellent observational skills.”

Emily tried to change the subject.

It did not work.

Across the room, Michael looked up.

Their eyes met.

He raised his hand in a small greeting.

Emily returned it.

Sarah saw everything.

She said nothing.

For approximately four seconds.

Then she whispered, “Oh, this is going to be interesting.”

Emily covered her face with one hand.

And for the first time in a long while, she found herself looking forward to Monday.
