# Chapter 18: The First Real Choice

The first university decision arrived on a Thursday, while Emily was sitting in the school cafeteria with a bowl of soup she had already decided not to finish.

Sarah saw the email notification appear on Emily's phone.

“Open it,” she said.

“I will.”

“Now.”

“I need a moment.”

“You have had three moments.”

Emily took a breath and opened the message.

The university had offered her a place in its literature and communications program, with an invitation to apply for a small creative-writing scholarship. She read the first paragraph, then the second, then started again from the beginning.

Sarah grabbed her hand.

“You got in.”

Emily nodded. The words seemed too simple for the size of the feeling. She had wanted this. She had worked for it. Still, when the possibility became real, it felt as if someone had opened a window in a room she had been living in for years.

Sarah stood and hugged her across the table.

“You did it.”

Emily laughed, then covered her face. “I did.”

The first person she called was Elizabeth. Her mother cried before Emily finished the sentence. James took the phone and asked whether the offer included financial support, then congratulated her with the seriousness of a man who believed practical questions were a form of affection.

Michael called ten minutes later.

“I heard,” he said.

“From whom?”

“Your father. He sent me a message that contained three exclamation marks. I assumed something significant had happened.”

Emily laughed. “I got in.”

“I know. I'm so proud of you.”

She closed her eyes. “Thank you.”

“Tell me everything.”

She read him the offer details. He asked about the scholarship, the program, and the campus. Then he asked the question she had been avoiding.

“Is it the one you want?”

“I think so.”

“Then that's the important part.”

“What if you get into a different university?”

“Then we'll compare the options.”

“What if they're far apart?”

“We'll talk about it.”

“What if—”

“Emily.”

She stopped.

“We don't have to solve every possible future today,” he said. “Today you got into a program you wanted. Let yourself be happy.”

She looked out the cafeteria window. Students were crossing the courtyard, carrying books and talking over one another. The ordinary world had continued, but something inside her had shifted.

“All right,” she said.

“Good. Now tell me what you want to do to celebrate.”

“Cake.”

“Excellent. I can support that plan.”

That evening, the families gathered at the Bennett house. Margaret arrived with a cake decorated with small blue flowers. Ashley brought a card that read, *For the future author who will eventually write an unflattering memoir about all of us.* David brought sparkling cider. James insisted on taking photographs, and Elizabeth kept asking Emily to read the acceptance email aloud.

The celebration was warm and noisy. Sarah came, followed by Sophia and Olivia. Madison sent flowers with a note congratulating Emily and wishing her well. Emily placed them beside the cake.

“Look at you,” Sophia said, surveying the room. “A successful writer already receiving flowers.”

“I haven't written anything successful yet.”

“You got into the program. Let us have the evening.”

Olivia asked Emily about the course structure and suggested a collection of essays she might enjoy. Sarah announced that she would be requesting a dedicated place in the acknowledgements of Emily's first book. Ashley demanded that the memoir include a chapter about Michael's disastrous skateboard ramp.

“I was not responsible for the shelf,” Michael said.

“You designed the ramp,” Ashley replied.

“I designed a concept.”

“You designed a trip to the emergency room.”

“An extremely minor emergency.”

Everyone laughed.

Later, when the guests had gone and the plates were stacked, Emily stepped into the garden. Michael followed her with two glasses of water.

“Are you tired?” he asked.

“Happy-tired.”

“That's a good kind.”

She took a glass. “I keep thinking about the decision.”

“Which one?”

“Whether to accept. Whether to apply for the scholarship. Whether I should wait to hear from the other programs.”

“You don't have to decide tonight.”

“I know. But I want to choose something because I want it, not because I'm afraid of losing something else.”

Michael leaned against the garden wall. “Then choose the place that helps you become the person you want to be.”

“What if that takes us in different directions?”

He looked at her carefully. “Then we will have to be honest about what we can build. I don't want either of us to give up a future just to avoid a difficult conversation.”

Emily felt the weight of the words. They were not a threat. They were a form of respect.

“I don't want you to give up anything either,” she said.

“Good.”

“Even if I miss you.”

“Especially then.”

She moved closer. “I think I love you more when you say sensible things.”

“That's unfortunate. I was hoping to be loved for my jokes.”

“You are loved for your jokes. Some of them.”

He put a hand against her cheek. “I love you for the way you make room for the truth, even when it's uncomfortable.”

She looked at him, and the garden seemed to grow still around them.

“I'm not always good at it,” she said.

“Neither am I.”

“Then we'll keep practicing.”

He kissed her. The kiss was soft, but it held a new kind of confidence. They were no longer simply imagining a future in which everything stayed familiar. They were beginning to choose the people they wanted to become, and to ask whether those people could continue choosing one another.

The next morning, Emily accepted the offer.

She did not do it because Michael had approved. She did not do it because her parents were proud. She did it because when she imagined herself in that program, she felt curious, challenged, and alive.

She applied for the scholarship and began preparing for the next stage.

Michael's university interview was scheduled for the following week. Emily helped him choose a shirt, then made him practice answering questions without turning every answer into a lecture about bridge design.

“Tell them about yourself,” she said.

“I like structural engineering.”

“That's not yourself. That's a subject.”

“I also like pancakes.”

“Michael.”

“What? It's important information.”

She laughed and adjusted his collar. “Tell them why you want to study engineering.”

He considered the question. “Because the built world affects people every day, whether they notice it or not. I want to help make it safer, more useful, and more welcoming.”

Emily smiled. “That's the answer.”

“Really?”

“Yes. And don't forget to breathe.”

“I'll try.”

He kissed her forehead before leaving for the interview.

That afternoon, Emily sat at her desk and wrote a new opening for her essay. She did not begin with a memory this time. She began with a choice: the moment when a person realizes that the life ahead of them cannot be built entirely from the life behind.

She wrote until the light changed.

When Michael called to say the interview had gone well, she listened to every detail. He had answered the questions, asked two of his own, and resisted the urge to explain the entire history of bridge engineering.

“I'm proud of you,” she said.

“I had a very good coach.”

“You had a very good shirt.”

“That too.”

They laughed.

Outside, evening settled over the street. Emily looked at the acceptance letter pinned above her desk and the photograph of Michael's bridge beside it. Two futures, both still taking shape.

She did not know exactly where they would lead.

For the first time, she understood that not knowing was not the same as being lost.
