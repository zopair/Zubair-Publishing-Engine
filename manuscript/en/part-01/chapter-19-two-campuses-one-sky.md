# Chapter 19: Two Campuses, One Sky

The university letters arrived over the next three weeks.

Michael was accepted into a civil engineering program in the city, with a place in a design studio that focused on public infrastructure. It was not the same university Emily had chosen, but the train journey between the campuses was manageable. They studied the schedules together at the kitchen table, moving paper timetables around as if they were pieces in a board game.

“Forty minutes,” Emily said.

“On a good day.”

“Fifty-five if the train is delayed.”

“An hour if the universe is personally offended.”

She drew a line between the two campuses on a map. “It's not impossible.”

“No,” Michael said. “It's not.”

They had expected the decision to feel like a test. Instead, it became a conversation about practical things: class schedules, travel costs, study time, and how often they could realistically see each other without turning every day into a race.

They made no grand promises. They agreed to protect time for one another, but also to make friends, join clubs, and let their lives grow beyond the relationship.

The summer before university became a season of preparation. Emily worked part-time at a local bookshop, where she learned how to recommend novels to strangers and how to recognize a customer who wanted company more than advice. Michael spent several weeks assisting at a small engineering office, mostly organizing drawings and observing site meetings. He came home with dust on his shoes and a new respect for people who knew how to build something from a plan.

One evening, he met Emily outside the bookshop after her shift.

“Have you eaten?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“Good. I have a plan.”

“Does it involve a bridge?”

“Not directly.”

He took her to a small restaurant they had passed many times but never entered. They sat by the window and ordered more food than they needed. Michael told her about a site engineer who could identify a measurement error from across a room. Emily described a customer who had spent twenty minutes asking for a book about loneliness, then bought a cookbook.

“Maybe she wanted to feel less alone,” Michael said.

“Maybe she wanted to feed someone.”

“That's another way.”

Emily watched him across the table. He had grown more confident, but not less curious. The world was giving him new questions, and he seemed happier for it.

“What?” he asked.

“I'm proud of you.”

He smiled. “You say that a lot.”

“I mean it every time.”

“I know.”

After dinner they walked toward the station. A light rain began, and Michael opened his umbrella. It was too small for two people, so Emily moved closer until their shoulders touched.

“Your umbrella is inadequate,” she said.

“It is an intimate umbrella.”

“It is a badly engineered umbrella.”

“I'll submit a redesign.”

She laughed and tucked her arm through his.

On the first day of university, Emily woke early and changed her outfit twice. Elizabeth pretended not to notice. James took photographs until Emily threatened to leave without him.

Michael sent a message before she boarded the train.

**Michael:** First day. You have this.  
**Emily:** I haven't even found the building.  
**Michael:** You will.  
**Emily:** What if I get lost?  
**Michael:** Ask someone. Or call me.  
**Emily:** What if you're in class?  
**Michael:** Then I will call you when I escape.

She smiled all the way to campus.

The first weeks were crowded with new names, lecture halls, reading lists, and the strange freedom of deciding where to spend an afternoon. Emily joined the student literary journal and attended a photography society meeting. She met Clara, a thoughtful student who wrote essays about family migration, and Ben, who could talk about poetry for an hour without repeating himself.

Michael's schedule was more demanding than he had expected. His design studio required long hours, and he joined a project group working on a pedestrian route near a busy station. He made friends with a student named Ethan, who had an alarming collection of measuring tools and an excellent sense of humour.

They did not see each other every day. Sometimes their calls were short. Sometimes one of them was too tired to talk. Once, Emily waited at the station for twenty minutes before learning that Michael's studio session had run late and his phone had died.

She was angry when he arrived.

“You could have found a way to tell me,” she said.

“I know. I'm sorry.”

“I was standing here wondering whether something had happened.”

“I should have asked Ethan to lend me his phone.”

“Yes.”

Michael did not defend himself. He stood beside her in the cold, accepting the fact that he had made her worry.

“I'll do better,” he said.

“I don't need you to be perfect. I need you to tell me when plans change.”

“I understand.”

She took a breath. “I'm glad you're here.”

He reached for her hand. “I'm glad too.”

They went to a nearby café, where the disagreement softened into a conversation about the pressures of their new routines. They agreed to send a quick message if either expected to be delayed, and to avoid treating a missed call as evidence of anything larger.

It was a small repair, but an important one.

By November, they had found a rhythm. Wednesday evenings were often theirs, even if it meant studying at the same table in the library. Saturdays were for longer visits when schedules allowed. They learned which train platforms were least crowded and which café made the best hot chocolate.

One evening, Emily showed Michael a short essay she had written for the journal. It was about the way a city changed when viewed from a train window.

He read it carefully.

“You made the station feel like a person,” he said.

“Is that good?”

“I think so. You noticed the people who wait there. The ones who arrive, leave, miss trains, meet someone.”

Emily smiled. “You would notice the bridges.”

“I would.”

He handed the pages back. “You should submit it.”

“I will.”

Outside the library windows, the city lights trembled in the rain. They packed their books and walked to the station together.

The two campuses were not the same place. Their days were not identical. But under the same sky, they were learning to make a life that could hold both ambition and affection.

The map between them had become more complicated.

It had also become theirs.
