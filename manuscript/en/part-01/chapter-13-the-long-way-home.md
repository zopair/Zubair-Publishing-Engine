# Chapter 13: The Long Way Home

The first week of Michael's summer program began with a train that was seven minutes late and a message from Emily that arrived just as he reached the platform.

**Emily:** Remember to eat breakfast.  
**Michael:** I have coffee.  
**Emily:** That is a beverage, not a breakfast.  
**Michael:** It contains ambition.  
**Emily:** It contains caffeine and poor judgment.  
**Michael:** I miss you already.  
**Emily:** You left twelve minutes ago.  
**Michael:** A difficult twelve minutes.

Emily read the exchange while sitting at her bedroom desk. She had an application essay due for a regional writing competition, two photography assignments, and a growing collection of messages from Michael that she was trying not to treat as a substitute for seeing him.

She put her phone down and returned to her draft.

The first few days were easier than she had expected. Michael sent photographs of the studio, the view from the train, and a paper model of a bridge that had collapsed under the weight of a pencil. He described the other students, the instructor who spoke in diagrams, and a classmate who had brought a ruler to lunch because he did not trust the cafeteria's portion sizes.

Emily sent him photographs from home: a crooked sunflower in the garden, Sarah asleep over a stack of newspapers, and a page of her essay with a question mark drawn beside a sentence she could not make work.

They developed a rhythm. A message in the morning. A call when the program ended. Sometimes a long conversation, sometimes only a few minutes before one of them had to leave. They were learning that affection did not need to fill every silence.

On Friday, Michael called later than usual.

“Did you forget I exist?” Emily asked.

“I tried. It was unsuccessful.”

“You sound tired.”

“I am. We had a design review.”

“Was it bad?”

“It was useful. Which is sometimes worse.”

He explained that the instructor had challenged his bridge design. The structure was elegant, but it did not account for a difficult wind condition. Michael had defended his calculations, then realized he had overlooked a practical constraint.

“I should have seen it,” he said.

“You did see it. Just later than you wanted.”

“That is a generous interpretation.”

“It is also accurate.”

He was quiet for a moment. “I hate being wrong in front of people.”

Emily leaned back in her chair. “You know what I hate?”

“What?”

“When you pretend a mistake means something about your worth.”

“I don't do that.”

“You absolutely do.”

“I may occasionally do that.”

“Michael.”

He exhaled. “Fine. I do.”

She smiled, though he could not see it. “You are allowed to learn in public.”

“That's a terrible phrase.”

“It's a good principle.”

He laughed. The tension in his voice loosened.

On Saturday, he came home for the weekend. Emily met him at the station, holding two coffees and a paper bag. When he stepped onto the platform, he looked tired enough to be older and happy enough to be himself.

“You brought breakfast,” he said.

“I brought food. You can decide what category it belongs to.”

He opened the bag. A cinnamon roll.

“Are you trying to make me emotionally dependent on pastry?”

“I'm trying to prevent you from surviving on coffee.”

He kissed her cheek, then took her hand.

They walked through town rather than taking the bus. Michael told her about the program, but he also asked about her essay. He remembered the paragraph she had struggled with and wanted to know whether she had solved it.

“I changed the whole opening,” she said.

“Was the old one bad?”

“It was trying too hard to sound important.”

“That's a serious offence.”

“I know. I sentenced it to deletion.”

They stopped outside a stationery shop. Emily went in to buy a new notebook. Michael followed, examining a display of mechanical pencils with the concentration of a jeweller.

“You have enough pencils,” she said.

“One can never have enough pencils.”

“You said the same thing about rulers.”

“Rulers are different.”

“How?”

“They are straight.”

She shook her head and paid for the notebook. As they left, Michael took it from her and wrote his name on the first page.

“What are you doing?”

“Claiming a small corner.”

“You're vandalizing my notebook.”

“Personalization.”

He added the date, then a small sketch of a bridge. Beneath it he wrote: *For the things still being built.*

Emily looked at the page longer than she intended.

That afternoon they sat in the park, reviewing her essay. Michael was careful not to turn the conversation into an engineering inspection. He asked what she wanted the reader to feel, where she was hiding the most important thought, and whether the ending was honest.

“You're annoyingly good at this,” she said.

“I have a gift for identifying structural weaknesses.”

“In essays?”

“In most things.”

She handed him the notebook. “Then tell me what you think.”

He read the final page twice.

“I think you should keep the sentence about your grandmother's kitchen,” he said. “It's the first time the piece feels like it belongs to you.”

Emily nodded. “I was afraid it was too ordinary.”

“Ordinary is where most of life happens.”

She looked at him. “You should write that down.”

“I just did.”

He tapped the notebook.

Before he returned to the city on Sunday evening, they stood on the station platform with the familiar train announcement echoing overhead. Emily had expected the goodbye to feel dramatic. Instead, it felt practical and tender: a hand held a little longer, a reminder about the next call, a promise to send photographs of the final model.

“Two weeks,” Michael said.

“Two weeks.”

“Then the presentation.”

“I'll be there.”

“I know.”

The train arrived. He stepped aboard, then turned back once before the doors closed.

“Emily?”

“Yes?”

“Thank you for not making me choose between missing you and doing something I care about.”

She held his gaze. “Thank you for letting me be part of it.”

The doors closed.

On the ride home, Emily opened the notebook to the first page. Michael's little bridge leaned across the margin, imperfect and charming. She ran her finger over the ink.

The long way home was not always the distance between stations. Sometimes it was the work of becoming someone who could love another person's future without abandoning her own.

She was learning the route.
