# Chapter 15: The Presentation Day

The final presentation took place in a glass-fronted building where every corridor seemed designed to make people walk faster.

Emily arrived twenty minutes early with Elizabeth and James. She had worn a soft green dress and carried her camera, though she had promised Michael she would not spend the entire afternoon behind it.

Margaret and David were already there. Margaret hugged Emily as if the presentation were a family occasion, which, by then, it was.

“Have you seen him?” she asked.

“Not yet.”

“He has been awake since five.”

“Michael?”

“No, the building. Michael has been awake since four.”

David laughed. “He was checking his model at breakfast.”

“He checked it before breakfast,” Margaret said. “Then he checked it while eating breakfast. I finally took the screwdriver away.”

Emily smiled. “He must be nervous.”

“He says he isn't.”

“Then he's nervous,” Ashley said, appearing behind them. She carried a small bouquet of yellow flowers and wore a black jacket with silver buttons. “He becomes very polite when he's nervous. It's unsettling.”

The group moved into the exhibition hall. Models stood on tables beneath bright lights. Posters displayed diagrams, measurements, and photographs of prototypes. Students moved between them, answering questions from instructors and visitors.

Emily found Michael near the far wall.

He was standing beside a model bridge made from wood, wire, and carefully measured supports. His shirt sleeves were rolled to his elbows. A pencil was tucked behind one ear. He was explaining a load distribution diagram to an instructor, speaking with the focused calm that came over him when he understood a problem deeply.

When he saw Emily, his face brightened.

“I'm glad you came.”

“I said I would.”

“I know. I still like seeing you arrive.”

She looked at the model. “It's beautiful.”

“It's unfinished.”

“It's a presentation model. It is allowed to be beautiful.”

He gave her a quick tour. The design had changed since the first sketch she had seen. He had widened the pedestrian path, altered the support angles, and added a flexible element to account for wind. He explained the choices without showing off. Emily asked questions, and he answered every one as if she were the most important person in the room.

“Does it work?” she asked.

“The model does.”

“And the real bridge?”

“Would need testing, permits, funding, and several people who know much more than I do.”

“So this is the beginning.”

“That's the idea.”

She looked at the small structure again. “You built something people could cross.”

“On a table.”

“Every bridge starts somewhere.”

He smiled. “You make it sound poetic.”

“I'm a writer. It's a professional hazard.”

The presentation began. Each student had a short time to explain the problem, the design, and the decisions that had shaped it. Michael spoke clearly. He described the original weakness in his first model and the criticism that had forced him to reconsider it.

Emily watched him acknowledge the mistake without apology or embarrassment. He explained what he had learned, then demonstrated how the revised structure distributed weight.

When he finished, the room applauded.

The instructor asked several questions. One concerned cost, another maintenance, and a third the experience of pedestrians using the bridge. Michael answered the first two carefully. For the third, he paused.

“I think a bridge should be safe and efficient,” he said. “But it should also make people feel that the distance between two places is something they can cross without fear. If the design works only on paper, then it hasn't done its job.”

Emily felt a quiet pride rise in her chest.

After the presentations, the students gathered while the instructors announced several distinctions. Michael received a commendation for design adaptation and practical problem-solving. He looked surprised when his name was called, then glanced toward his family.

Margaret clapped so loudly that Ashley covered one ear.

“That's my son,” Margaret said, as if the entire hall had somehow missed the information.

Michael returned to them holding a certificate. Margaret hugged him first. David followed, then Ashley, who pretended to inspect the certificate for spelling errors.

Emily waited until the family had finished.

“Well?” Michael asked.

“Well what?”

“Your professional assessment.”

She pretended to study the certificate. “The handwriting is acceptable. The design is promising. The engineer is tolerable.”

“Tolerable?”

“On a good day.”

He laughed, then took her hand. “Thank you for coming.”

“I wouldn't have missed it.”

He leaned closer. “I know. But I wanted to say it.”

They stepped outside with the others. The afternoon sun had turned the glass building gold. The families decided to celebrate at a nearby restaurant. On the walk there, Michael fell behind with Emily.

“Did you like it?” he asked.

“I loved it.”

“Even the diagrams?”

“Especially the diagrams.”

“Liar.”

“Fine. I liked the person explaining them.”

He smiled and brushed his shoulder against hers.

At the restaurant, the adults occupied one end of the table while the younger group gathered at the other. Sarah had joined them after her own appointment and immediately demanded to see photographs. Sophia asked Michael about the model's materials. Olivia wanted to know whether the design could be adapted for a public-space project. Madison, who had attended the presentation after hearing about it through the program, asked a thoughtful question about accessibility.

Michael answered each person in turn. He did not seem to mind the attention, but he did not perform for it either. When the conversation turned to the summer exhibition, he invited Emily to help choose photographs for the presentation board.

“Only if she wants to,” he added.

“I want to,” Emily said.

Madison nodded. “Your photographs at the school exhibition were excellent. You have a good eye for people in a space.”

“Thank you,” Emily said, and meant it.

The conversation moved on. For once, Emily did not feel the need to examine every glance or weigh every compliment. There was enough room at the table for everyone to be talented, ambitious, and human.

Later, as the families were leaving, Michael found Emily beside the restaurant window.

“I have something for you,” he said.

He handed her a small envelope. Inside was a photograph from the first day of the program: Michael standing in the studio beside his earliest bridge sketch, looking tired and uncertain. On the back he had written:

*For the person who reminded me that a first draft is allowed to be imperfect.*

Emily turned the photograph over again.

“You kept this?”

“I kept it because you were right.”

“I am often right.”

“Let's not make this difficult.”

She laughed, then slipped the photograph into her bag.

“Your turn,” he said.

“My turn for what?”

“To give me something.”

“I didn't bring a gift.”

“You brought yourself.”

“That is a very unfair way to win an argument.”

“I learned from the best.”

She stood on her toes and kissed him. It was brief, warm, and full of everything she had wanted to say since the acceptance email arrived.

When they parted, Michael rested his forehead against hers.

“Next summer,” he said, “I want to build something bigger.”

“Another bridge?”

“Maybe. Or something that helps people use a city more easily.”

“You'll have to tell me about it.”

“I will.”

“And I will ask too many questions.”

“I expect nothing less.”

The train ride home was full of conversation. Margaret and Elizabeth compared notes about the presentation. David asked James whether he knew anyone who could advise Michael about university applications. Ashley teased her brother about his speech. Emily sat beside Michael, their hands joined beneath the table.

She looked around at the people gathered close to them and thought of the bridge on the exhibition table. It was not finished. It did not yet carry anyone across a river. But it had already done something important: it had brought a group of people into one room, made them proud, and given them a reason to imagine what might come next.

That night, Emily placed Michael's photograph beside the notebook he had given her.

She wrote one sentence before going to sleep:

*Some achievements are measured by what we build. Others by the people who come to stand beside us when we show it to the world.*

Then she turned off the light, carrying the sound of the applause into her dreams.
