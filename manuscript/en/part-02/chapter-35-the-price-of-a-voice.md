# Chapter 35: The Price of a Voice

Victor Hayes first contacted Emily after reading a short article about the family archive pilot.

He was an investor who specialized in technology companies with strong consumer engagement. He spoke warmly, listened closely, and seemed to understand the emotional significance of the project.

They met in a quiet office overlooking the city.

“I think what you're building could help millions of people,” he said. “The market is much larger than you realize.”

Emily kept her notebook closed. “We're not trying to build a market around grief.”

Victor smiled. “Of course not. But people need these services. There is a real opportunity to make the experience accessible.”

Daniel, seated beside her, asked what kind of service Victor imagined.

“An interactive memory companion,” Victor replied. “A conversational experience that feels natural. People should be able to speak to the person they miss, not just search through folders.”

Emily looked at him. “That is not what we are building.”

“Not yet.”

“We don't recreate people.”

“You are being too cautious. The emotional connection is the value.”

“The emotional connection belongs to the family. It is not a feature to maximize.”

Victor folded his hands. “I understand the concern. But if users spend more time with the platform, they receive more value.”

“Not necessarily,” Emily said. “More time could mean greater dependence. We need to design for healthy use, including the ability to stop.”

Victor's expression remained pleasant, but his questions became more pointed. He asked about retention, subscription tiers, and whether families would pay for a more lifelike voice. He suggested that the system could proactively send messages on birthdays and anniversaries.

Emily said no.

After the meeting, Daniel walked with her toward the station.

“He could fund the infrastructure,” Daniel said.

“He wants a different product.”

“He might accept safeguards.”

“He called emotional dependence engagement.”

Daniel did not argue.

A week later, Victor sent a proposal. It included a new interface with a generated avatar, a voice model trained on family recordings, and a feature that would send unsolicited messages during periods of low activity. The document described these messages as “re-engagement moments.”

Emily read the phrase twice.

She declined the proposal.

Victor requested another meeting. This time, he was less warm.

“You are turning down an opportunity to scale something meaningful,” he said.

“We are turning down a design that could mislead grieving people.”

“Users understand the difference between software and reality.”

“Some will. Some won't. Grief changes how people interpret things. We cannot pretend that a disclaimer solves every problem.”

Victor leaned back. “If you refuse to innovate, someone else will build it.”

“Then someone else will have to answer for what they build.”

The meeting ended politely. The disagreement did not.

Two days later, Emily discovered that a demonstration environment had generated a response using a family recording without the source label appearing on the screen. The response included a sentence that Michael had never said. It was plausible, warm, and entirely invented.

Emily felt cold.

Daniel traced the issue. A test configuration had allowed the language model to combine fragments from separate recordings without showing the provenance panel. The output sounded convincing, but there was no evidence that Michael had ever expressed the thought.

They disabled the feature immediately.

Margaret was informed before anyone else. Emily explained what had happened and showed her the generated sentence.

Margaret read it silently.

“Did he say this?” she asked.

“No. We cannot verify it.”

“Then don't show it to me as his words.”

“We won't.”

Margaret handed the tablet back. “I would rather hear one true recording than a hundred beautiful lies.”

Emily felt the sentence strike deeper than any technical review.

They held an emergency meeting with the pilot families. They explained the error, identified the affected test data, removed the generated output, and documented the incident. They paused conversational generation entirely until they could guarantee that every response would distinguish sourced material from generated language and provide traceable evidence.

Some families were disappointed. One withdrew from the pilot. Another asked for more control over what could be processed. Emily accepted both decisions.

The project became smaller for a while. It also became more trustworthy.

Victor sent one final message, suggesting that they reconsider once the market had matured.

Emily did not reply.

That evening, she visited Margaret. The tablet was on the dining table, its screen dark.

“I am sorry,” Emily said.

Margaret looked at her. “You did not invent the sentence.”

“But I built the system that allowed it.”

“Then you corrected it.”

“We should have caught it sooner.”

“Yes,” Margaret said. “You should have.”

Emily accepted the criticism.

Margaret took her hand. “Do not let your guilt make you abandon the good work. Just remember what it is for.”

Emily looked at the tablet.

“What do you want it to be?” she asked.

“A place where I can find his voice when I want to hear it. A place where Ashley can add her stories. A place where the grandchildren I may never meet can see what he was like. But I do not want a machine to tell me what my son would say today.”

Emily nodded.

“Then that is the line we will keep.”

On the way home, Emily called Daniel.

“We need a stronger architecture,” she said. “Source-bound retrieval only for anything presented as a memory. Generated summaries clearly labeled. No autonomous messages. No emotional scoring. No features designed to keep someone online.”

Daniel was quiet for a moment.

“It's more work.”

“I know.”

“Then let's do it properly.”

They began again.
