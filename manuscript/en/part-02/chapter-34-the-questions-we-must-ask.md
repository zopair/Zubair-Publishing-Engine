# Chapter 34: The Questions We Must Ask

Daniel arrived at the apartment carrying a laptop, a notebook, and a list of questions.

He had spent the week reviewing the archive prototype. The search worked, the source labels were clear, and the tablet interface was simple enough for Margaret to use without assistance. But he had also found several problems they needed to address before sharing it with anyone else.

“First,” he said, sitting at the kitchen table, “we need to decide who owns the archive.”

“The family,” Emily replied.

“Which members of the family?”

She paused.

Daniel opened his notebook. “Michael's photographs include other people. His messages include private conversations. His recordings may contain information about people who never agreed to be part of a digital archive.”

Emily looked at the screen. She had been thinking about the material as something Michael had left behind. Daniel was reminding her that some of it had been created with other people.

“We need permissions,” she said.

“And a way to remove material.”

“Yes.”

“And a way to stop the system from generating anything from a source that someone has withdrawn.”

Emily nodded. “Let's build that into the design before we add features.”

They spent the afternoon mapping the archive's rules. Original files would remain unchanged. Each item would have a source, date if known, and permission status. Family members could choose which items were shared with whom. Private messages would not be included by default. A contributor could withdraw their own material. Any generated summary would be visibly labeled and linked to the sources it used.

Daniel added another requirement: the archive would never claim to reproduce the person.

“We can make the language warmer,” he said, “but we should not make the system pretend it is Michael.”

Emily looked at him. “Agreed.”

A week later, she brought the prototype to a small family meeting. Margaret, David, Ashley, Elizabeth, James, Sarah, and Olivia attended. Madison joined by video because she was coordinating an event.

Emily explained what they had built and what it could not do.

“It can organize photographs, recordings, and stories,” she said. “It can search them and show where each item came from. It cannot bring Michael back. It does not know what he would think today. It cannot answer questions for which there is no evidence.”

Margaret listened carefully.

“Could it help me find the recording where he talks about the first time he went to the sea?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Could it tell me what he said?”

“If the recording contains it. Otherwise, it will tell us it couldn't find it.”

Margaret nodded. “That is honest.”

Ashley asked whether she could contribute her own stories. Emily explained that personal recollections could be added, but they would be labeled as Ashley's memories rather than facts verified by Michael.

“I like that,” Ashley said. “I don't want my version to become the official version.”

David asked about security. Daniel explained the access controls and the need for backups. Olivia asked how they would handle a family member who wanted to stop participating. Emily said that consent had to remain ongoing, not something granted once and forgotten.

The meeting lasted two hours. They disagreed about several details, but the disagreements made the system better.

After everyone left, Margaret stayed behind.

“I want to ask you something,” she said.

Emily waited.

“Do you think this is helping me remember him, or helping me avoid that he is gone?”

Emily took a breath. It was the most important question anyone had asked.

“I don't know how to measure that for you,” she said. “But I don't want the archive to become a place where you have to stay. I want it to be something you can visit, and leave, and return to when it helps.”

Margaret looked down at her hands.

“I don't want to forget him,” she said.

“You won't.”

“But I also don't want to spend the rest of my life sitting in his room.”

Emily reached for her hand. “Then we will make sure the archive supports your life, not replaces it.”

Margaret looked at her for a long moment.

“Your mother is lucky to have you,” she said.

Emily shook her head. “I'm lucky to have both of you.”

The following month, Emily and Daniel invited a small group of families to test the archive with their own materials. They did not call it a product. They called it a pilot, and every participant received a clear explanation of what would happen to their files.

One family used it to organize photographs of a grandmother who had died years earlier. Another wanted to preserve a father's recorded stories for grandchildren who had never met him. Their needs were different, and their boundaries were different.

Emily listened more than she spoke.

She began to understand that the archive was not a single emotional experience. For some people, it was a family library. For others, it was a way to organize scattered recordings. Some wanted to hear a familiar voice occasionally. Others wanted no simulated interaction at all.

The system had to make room for all of them.

At the end of the pilot, Daniel looked at the feedback.

“We have something useful,” he said.

Emily looked at the families' notes. “We have the beginning of something useful.”

He smiled. “You're learning to be careful with success.”

“I'm learning that people trust us with things we cannot replace.”

She closed the notebook.

The questions were not obstacles to the work.

They were part of the work.
