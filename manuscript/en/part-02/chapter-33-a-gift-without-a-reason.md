# Chapter 33: A Gift Without a Reason

The package arrived on a Wednesday afternoon.

Emily found it outside the apartment door when she returned from work. It was small, wrapped in brown paper, and addressed in Michael's handwriting.

She carried it inside.

Michael was still at the office, so she sent him a photograph.

**Emily:** Should I be concerned?  
**Michael:** No.  
**Emily:** That is exactly what someone says before a surprise.  
**Michael:** Open it.  
**Emily:** Is it alive?  
**Michael:** Not unless stationery has evolved.

Inside was a small wooden box. It contained a collection of printed photographs, each one carefully selected and arranged by date. There were pictures from the school library, the river, the summer program, the university exhibition, their first apartment, and the wedding.

At the bottom was a small memory card and a note.

*I wanted to give you something that didn't need a special occasion. These are the moments I keep returning to. There are more, but I thought we could add them together.*

Emily sat on the sofa and looked through the photographs.

The earliest picture showed Michael with a pencil case open at his feet. Another showed Emily laughing at the river. There was a photograph of the two mothers standing together at the wedding, and one of Margaret holding Emily's hand during the dress appointment.

The last photograph was from the previous weekend. Emily was asleep on the sofa with a book open on her chest. Michael had taken it from the armchair, and she looked peaceful.

She smiled.

When he came home, she was waiting near the door.

“You made this?”

He nodded.

“Why?”

“Because I wanted to.”

She held the box against her chest. “It's beautiful.”

“I thought you might like it.”

“I love it.”

He took off his coat. “I was worried it might be too sentimental.”

“It's exactly sentimental enough.”

She showed him the photographs again, stopping at the one from the school library.

“Look at us,” she said.

“We had no idea.”

“Did you?”

“Not then.”

She looked at the wedding photograph. “I like that the story is still growing.”

Michael sat beside her. “That's why I left space in the box.”

They spent the evening adding captions to the photographs. Some were accurate; others were arguments waiting to happen.

Emily wrote beneath the river photograph: *The day Michael negotiated unsuccessfully with the weather.*

He added beneath the school picture: *The day Emily laughed at a serious engineering mind.*

She crossed out *serious* and wrote *overconfident*.

He objected. She refused to change it.

Later, they copied the photographs to the memory card and stored a second copy on a drive. Michael explained that he wanted the collection to be safe, not because he expected anything to happen, but because the images mattered.

Emily thought about how carefully he treated the small pieces of their history.

“You always want to preserve things,” she said.

“Not everything.”

“Photographs. Drawings. Notes. Receipts with sketches on them.”

“Receipts are important.”

She smiled. “Why?”

“Because sometimes they contain the beginning of an idea.”

She leaned against him. “I think people are like that too. We keep pieces of one another.”

Michael considered the thought. “Maybe. But people are more than the pieces we keep.”

Emily looked at him.

He continued, “A photograph can remind you of someone. It can't contain them.”

She nodded. “I know.”

The sentence seemed ordinary then. It was simply a thoughtful distinction between memory and presence.

They put the box on the bookshelf, beside the wedding photograph and the small notebook from their first anniversary.

Before bed, Emily wrote in her own notebook:

*There are gifts that arrive because a date asks for them. And there are gifts that arrive because someone has been paying attention.*

She paused, then added:

*The best ones make an ordinary Wednesday feel like a place worth remembering.*

Michael came into the room and found her writing.

“Another essay?”

“Maybe.”

“About me?”

“Possibly.”

“Should I be worried?”

“Not unless you have been behaving badly.”

He laughed and kissed her forehead.

They turned off the lights. Outside, the city continued with its usual traffic and distant voices.

The wooden box remained on the shelf, holding photographs of a life that was still unfolding.

Neither of them knew how important that small collection would become.
